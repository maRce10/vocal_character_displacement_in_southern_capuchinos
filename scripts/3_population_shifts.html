Purpose

  • Evaluate whether populations that co-occur with a congener are acoustically closer to (convergence) or farther from (divergence) that congener’s song than conspecific populations that do not co-occur with it

The sympatry models (Step 2) show whether heterospecific songs are more or less different in sympatry, but a dyadic comparison cannot tell which species changed. Here we ask, for each focal species, whether populations that co-occur with a given congener are acoustically closer to (convergence) or farther from (divergence) that congener’s song than conspecific populations that do not co-occur with it.

  • Pairs are always oriented as focal song × congener song.
  • Pairs recorded at the same locality (the sympatric comparisons used in Step 2) are excluded. Congener songs therefore act as an external reference, and this test does not reuse the Step 2 contrast.
  • cooccur = 1 when the focal population’s locality also has the congener.
  • A shift can only be tested when the focal species has at least one population with the congener and at least one without it, and when the congener was also recorded at a locality other than the co-occurring focal population(s). Combinations that fail either condition are listed but not modelled. For example, iberaensis and palustris were recorded only at E_Ibera. Once same-locality pairs are removed, the E_Ibera hypoxantha population has no congener songs to be compared with, so hypoxantha vs. iberaensis and hypoxantha vs. palustris cannot be tested this way.

\[ \begin{split} \text{acoustic distance}_{\text{focal, congener}} &\sim \text{cooccur} \\ &\quad + (1 \mid \text{focal population}) + (1 \mid \text{congener population}) \\ &\quad + (1 \mid \text{focal individual}) + (1 \mid \text{congener individual}) \end{split} \]

Because pairs are oriented, focal and congener populations (and individuals) enter as separate crossed random effects rather than as multi-membership terms. The co-occurrence effect is a property of focal populations. It is therefore evaluated against the variation among focal populations (sd(pop1)), which is the appropriate and conservative unit of replication given the very small number of populations involved. The response is standardized within each focal–congener dataset, with the same regularizing priors as in Step 1.

Code 
shift_models_simple <- list()
shift_dat_simple <- list()

for (k in which(shift_combos_simple$testable)) {

  focal <- shift_combos_simple$focal[k]
  congener <- shift_combos_simple$congener[k]
  nm <- paste(focal, "vs", congener, sep = "_")

  shift_dat_simple[[nm]] <- population_shift_data(
    pairs = pairs_simple,
    focal = focal,
    congener = congener,
    occ = occ_simple
  )

  shift_models_simple[[nm]] <- brm(
    acoustic_distance_sc ~ cooccur +
      (1 | pop1) + (1 | pop2) +
      (1 | individual1) + (1 | individual2),
    data = shift_dat_simple[[nm]],
    family = gaussian(),
    prior = priors_ws,
    cores = 4,
    chains = 4,
    iter = 10000,
    backend = "cmdstanr",
    threads = threading(8),
    control = list(adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 15),
    file = paste0("./data/processed/fits/population_shift_", nm, "_simple_fit")
  )
}

shift_plot_dat_simple <- do.call(rbind, lapply(names(shift_dat_simple), function(nm) {
  x <- shift_dat_simple[[nm]]
  agg <- do.call(rbind, lapply(split(x, x$focal_population), function(p) {
    data.frame(
      focal_population = p$focal_population[1],
      cooccur = p$cooccur[1],
      mean = mean(p$acoustic_distance),
      se = sd(p$acoustic_distance) / sqrt(nrow(p))
    )
  }))
  agg$comparison <- gsub("_vs_", " vs. ", nm)
  agg
}))

Summary

Model summary

  • Only two of the twelve focal-congener combinations have enough data to test (populations with and without the congener, plus congener reference populations elsewhere): Ruficollis vs. Hypoxantha and the reverse, Hypoxantha vs. Ruficollis. Neither co-occurrence effect is credible (Ruficollis vs. Hypoxantha: -0.228 SD, 95% CI [-0.778, 0.405]; Hypoxantha vs. Ruficollis: 0.004 SD, 95% CI [-0.537, 0.554]).
  • The Hypoxantha vs. Ruficollis model shows a convergence warning (Rhat = 1.094) and a high divergent-transition rate (3128/20,000 draws, 15.6%), so its null result should be treated as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no shift. The Ruficollis vs. Hypoxantha model converged cleanly (Rhat = 1.003, 146/20,000 divergent, 0.7%) and its null result is more trustworthy.
  • Expressed relative to the typical between-population difference for the focal species, both estimates are extremely imprecise (Ruficollis vs. Hypoxantha: -0.780, 95% CI [-5.768, 0.874]; Hypoxantha vs. Ruficollis: 0.005, 95% CI [-6.764, 4.826]) — the data cannot rule out a shift as large as several ordinary population differences in either direction.

Interpretation

  • With only one species pair testable and very few populations per comparison, Step 3 has essentially no statistical power for simple songs: the absence of a credible shift reflects data scarcity more than evidence against population-level convergence or divergence.
  • The point estimates are at least directionally consistent with the Step 2 sympatry pattern: Ruficollis populations that co-occur with Hypoxantha trend towards acoustic convergence (negative shift) rather than divergence, the opposite of what simple-song character displacement in Step 2 would predict if it operated through this specific species’ shift; given the width of the interval, however, this is only a weak hint and not a finding.

Summary

Model summary

  • As for simple songs, only Ruficollis vs. Hypoxantha and Hypoxantha vs. Ruficollis are testable. Neither co-occurrence effect is credible (Ruficollis vs. Hypoxantha: -0.346 SD, 95% CI [-0.980, 0.516]; Hypoxantha vs. Ruficollis: 0.052 SD, 95% CI [-0.537, 0.576]), and both models converged cleanly (Rhat = 1, divergent transitions below 1% in both).
  • Relative to the typical between-population difference, both estimates are again extremely imprecise (Ruficollis vs. Hypoxantha: -1.450, 95% CI [-51.529, 0.908]; Hypoxantha vs. Ruficollis: 0.379, 95% CI [-4.070, 18.238]).

Interpretation

  • As with simple songs, Step 3 provides no credible evidence of population-level convergence or divergence for complex songs, but this is a statement about statistical power (one testable species pair, few populations) rather than about biology.
  • Unlike the simple-song Hypoxantha vs. Ruficollis model, both complex-song models converged cleanly, so these null results are not undermined by sampling problems — they are genuinely uninformative given the data available, not masked by a convergence failure.
  • Taken together with Step 2, the strongest and best-supported evidence for sound-based character displacement in this system remains the heterospecific sympatric-vs-allopatric contrast (Step 2), not the population-shift framing used here, which is limited by how few species pairs have the co-occurrence structure needed to test it.

# Purpose {.unnumbered .unlisted}

-   Evaluate whether populations that co-occur with a congener are acoustically closer to (convergence) or farther from (divergence) that congener's song than conspecific populations that do not co-occur with it
:::

The sympatry models (Step 2) show whether heterospecific songs are more or less different in sympatry, but a dyadic comparison cannot tell which species changed. Here we ask, for each focal species, whether populations that co-occur with a given congener are acoustically closer to (convergence) or farther from (divergence) that congener's song than conspecific populations that do not co-occur with it.

-   Pairs are always oriented as focal song × congener song.
-   Pairs recorded at the same locality (the sympatric comparisons used in Step 2) are excluded. Congener songs therefore act as an external reference, and this test does not reuse the Step 2 contrast.
-   `cooccur = 1` when the focal population's locality also has the congener.
-   A shift can only be tested when the focal species has at least one population with the congener and at least one without it, and when the congener was also recorded at a locality other than the co-occurring focal population(s). Combinations that fail either condition are listed but not modelled. For example, iberaensis and palustris were recorded only at E_Ibera. Once same-locality pairs are removed, the E_Ibera hypoxantha population has no congener songs to be compared with, so hypoxantha vs. iberaensis and hypoxantha vs. palustris cannot be tested this way.

$$
\begin{split}
\text{acoustic distance}_{\text{focal, congener}} &\sim \text{cooccur} \\
&\quad + (1 \mid \text{focal population}) + (1 \mid \text{congener population}) \\
&\quad + (1 \mid \text{focal individual}) + (1 \mid \text{congener individual})
\end{split}
$$

Because pairs are oriented, focal and congener populations (and individuals) enter as separate crossed random effects rather than as multi-membership terms. The co-occurrence effect is a property of focal populations. It is therefore evaluated against the variation among focal populations (`sd(pop1)`), which is the appropriate and conservative unit of replication given the very small number of populations involved. The response is standardized within each focal–congener dataset, with the same regularizing priors as in Step 1.

```{r population shift models simple}

shift_models_simple <- list()
shift_dat_simple <- list()

for (k in which(shift_combos_simple$testable)) {

  focal <- shift_combos_simple$focal[k]
  congener <- shift_combos_simple$congener[k]
  nm <- paste(focal, "vs", congener, sep = "_")

  shift_dat_simple[[nm]] <- population_shift_data(
    pairs = pairs_simple,
    focal = focal,
    congener = congener,
    occ = occ_simple
  )

  shift_models_simple[[nm]] <- brm(
    acoustic_distance_sc ~ cooccur +
      (1 | pop1) + (1 | pop2) +
      (1 | individual1) + (1 | individual2),
    data = shift_dat_simple[[nm]],
    family = gaussian(),
    prior = priors_ws,
    cores = 4,
    chains = 4,
    iter = 10000,
    backend = "cmdstanr",
    threads = threading(8),
    control = list(adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 15),
    file = paste0("./data/processed/fits/population_shift_", nm, "_simple_fit")
  )
}
```

## Summary {.unnumbered .unlisted}

### Model summary {.unnumbered .unlisted}

-   Only two of the twelve focal-congener combinations have enough data to test (populations with and without the congener, plus congener reference populations elsewhere): *Ruficollis* vs. *Hypoxantha* and the reverse, *Hypoxantha* vs. *Ruficollis*. Neither co-occurrence effect is credible (*Ruficollis* vs. *Hypoxantha*: -0.228 SD, 95% CI [-0.778, 0.405]; *Hypoxantha* vs. *Ruficollis*: 0.004 SD, 95% CI [-0.537, 0.554]).
-   The *Hypoxantha* vs. *Ruficollis* model shows a convergence warning (Rhat = 1.094) and a high divergent-transition rate (3128/20,000 draws, 15.6%), so its null result should be treated as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no shift. The *Ruficollis* vs. *Hypoxantha* model converged cleanly (Rhat = 1.003, 146/20,000 divergent, 0.7%) and its null result is more trustworthy.
-   Expressed relative to the typical between-population difference for the focal species, both estimates are extremely imprecise (*Ruficollis* vs. *Hypoxantha*: -0.780, 95% CI [-5.768, 0.874]; *Hypoxantha* vs. *Ruficollis*: 0.005, 95% CI [-6.764, 4.826]) — the data cannot rule out a shift as large as several ordinary population differences in either direction.

### Interpretation {.unnumbered .unlisted}

-   With only one species pair testable and very few populations per comparison, Step 3 has essentially no statistical power for simple songs: the absence of a credible shift reflects data scarcity more than evidence against population-level convergence or divergence.
-   The point estimates are at least directionally consistent with the Step 2 sympatry pattern: *Ruficollis* populations that co-occur with *Hypoxantha* trend towards acoustic convergence (negative shift) rather than divergence, the opposite of what simple-song character displacement in Step 2 would predict if it operated through this specific species' shift; given the width of the interval, however, this is only a weak hint and not a finding.
:::

## Summary {.unnumbered .unlisted}

### Model summary {.unnumbered .unlisted}

-   As for simple songs, only *Ruficollis* vs. *Hypoxantha* and *Hypoxantha* vs. *Ruficollis* are testable. Neither co-occurrence effect is credible (*Ruficollis* vs. *Hypoxantha*: -0.346 SD, 95% CI [-0.980, 0.516]; *Hypoxantha* vs. *Ruficollis*: 0.052 SD, 95% CI [-0.537, 0.576]), and both models converged cleanly (Rhat = 1, divergent transitions below 1% in both).
-   Relative to the typical between-population difference, both estimates are again extremely imprecise (*Ruficollis* vs. *Hypoxantha*: -1.450, 95% CI [-51.529, 0.908]; *Hypoxantha* vs. *Ruficollis*: 0.379, 95% CI [-4.070, 18.238]).

### Interpretation {.unnumbered .unlisted}

-   As with simple songs, Step 3 provides no credible evidence of population-level convergence or divergence for complex songs, but this is a statement about statistical power (one testable species pair, few populations) rather than about biology.
-   Unlike the simple-song *Hypoxantha* vs. *Ruficollis* model, both complex-song models converged cleanly, so these null results are not undermined by sampling problems — they are genuinely uninformative given the data available, not masked by a convergence failure.
-   Taken together with Step 2, the strongest and best-supported evidence for sound-based character displacement in this system remains the heterospecific sympatric-vs-allopatric contrast (Step 2), not the population-shift framing used here, which is limited by how few species pairs have the co-occurrence structure needed to test it.
