1 Simple songs

1.1 Statistical analysis

To evaluate whether acoustic divergence between heterospecific songs differed between sympatric and allopatric population comparisons, we fitted a Bayesian mixed-effects model while accounting for the non-independence inherent to pairwise distance data.

The model was specified as:

\[ \begin{split} \text{acoustic distance} &\sim \text{sympatry} \\ &\quad + (1 \mid \text{species pair}) \\ &\quad + (1 \mid \text{mm(population}_1,\text{population}_2)) \\ &\quad + (1 \mid \text{mm(individual}_1,\text{individual}_2)) \end{split} \]

where:

  • (_{ij}) is the Euclidean distance between songs (i) and (j) in multivariate acoustic space.

  • (_{ij}) is a binary predictor indicating whether the populations from which songs (i) and (j) were recorded occur in sympatry (1) or allopatry (0).

  • species pair is a random intercept accounting for baseline differences in acoustic divergence among heterospecific species combinations.

  • mm(population(_1), population(_2)) is a multi-membership random effect accounting for repeated use of the same populations across pairwise comparisons.

  • mm(individual(_1), individual(_2)) is a multi-membership random effect accounting for repeated use of the same individuals across pairwise comparisons.

Model specifications:

  • The model was fitted in a Bayesian framework using the brms package, with sympatry as the only fixed effect. Geographic distance was not included: sympatric comparisons are all within-locality and allopatric comparisons all between localities, so sympatry and geographic distance cannot be separated with this sampling (posterior correlation between their coefficients of about 0.9 in the models that included both). Because isolation by distance predicts greater similarity at short distances, omitting geographic distance should, if anything, make the sympatry effect conservative when acoustic distance increases with geographic distance.
  • The response variable was pairwise Euclidean acoustic distance and was modeled using a Gaussian error distribution.
  • Only heterospecific comparisons were included.
  • Analyses were restricted to species pairs for which both sympatric and allopatric population comparisons were available. This restriction ensured that sympatry effects were estimated within the same species-pair contrasts rather than being confounded by species pairs occurring exclusively in sympatry or exclusively in allopatry.
  • Species-pair identity was included as a random intercept to account for inherent differences in acoustic divergence among species combinations.
  • Population identity was modeled using a multi-membership random effect because each pairwise comparison simultaneously involves two populations, and each population contributes to multiple pairwise distances.
  • Individual identity was modeled using a multi-membership random effect because each pairwise comparison simultaneously involves two individuals, and each individual contributes to multiple pairwise distances.
  • Only unique pairwise comparisons were retained and self-comparisons were excluded.
  • Weakly informative, regularizing priors were specified for all parameters.
  • The model was fitted using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo as implemented in Stan through the cmdstanr backend.

The coefficient for sympatry in this model represents the difference in acoustic divergence between sympatric (within-locality) and allopatric (between-locality) heterospecific comparisons, after accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data. The same model structure was fitted separately for the overall PCA-based acoustic distance and for each individual acoustic feature, so that trait-specific effects of sympatry could be examined alongside the overall pattern.

1.1.1.1.5 Divergent transitions

The sampler produced divergent transitions in this model. Divergences mark regions of the posterior that Hamiltonian Monte Carlo could not explore reliably, so estimates from those regions may be biased (Betancourt 2017). Without refitting, the checks below show (1) how many post-warmup draws diverged and whether they are concentrated in one chain, (2) whether divergences pile up at small values of any random-effect SD (a “funnel”; the species-pair SD, estimated from only three levels, is the most likely candidate), and (3) whether the fixed-effect estimates differ between divergent and non-divergent draws. If divergences are confined to a random-effect SD and the fixed effects look the same in both sets of draws, the inference on sympatry is unlikely to be affected. These checks cannot show what the sampler failed to explore, so refitting (e.g. with species pair as a fixed effect) remains the definitive test.

Model summary
  • Sympatric heterospecific pairs have credibly greater PCA-based acoustic distance than allopatric pairs (sympatry1 = 0.227 SD, 95% CI [0.204, 0.250]), with clean convergence (Rhat = 1, no CIs near zero).

Interpretation
  • Simple songs of heterospecific pairs are more acoustically different where the species co-occur than where they do not — the signature predicted by sound-based character displacement (selection against acoustic confusion in sympatry, e.g. via reproductive interference or mistaken aggression).
1.1.1.1.6 Contrasts among population pairs

Using the fitted model as is, the expected acoustic distance is computed for every observed combination of populations within each species pair, including the species-pair and population (multi-membership) effects and setting the individual effects to zero (i.e. for an average individual). Sympatric population pairs (the two species recorded at the same locality) are then contrasted against each allopatric population pair of the same species pair; each contrast is computed draw by draw, so its uncertainty interval reflects the joint posterior. Contrasts that share a population pair (e.g. the same sympatric pair against several allopatric ones) are not independent of each other and should be read as a set rather than as separate tests.

2.1 Statistical analysis

To evaluate whether acoustic divergence between heterospecific songs differed between sympatric and allopatric population comparisons, we fitted a Bayesian mixed-effects model while accounting for the non-independence inherent to pairwise distance data.

The model was specified as:

\[ \begin{split} \text{acoustic distance} &\sim \text{sympatry} \\ &\quad + (1 \mid \text{species pair}) \\ &\quad + (1 \mid \text{mm(population}_1,\text{population}_2)) \\ &\quad + (1 \mid \text{mm(individual}_1,\text{individual}_2)) \end{split} \]

where:

  • (_{ij}) is the Euclidean distance between songs (i) and (j) in multivariate acoustic space.

  • (_{ij}) is a binary predictor indicating whether the populations from which songs (i) and (j) were recorded occur in sympatry (1) or allopatry (0).

  • species pair is a random intercept accounting for baseline differences in acoustic divergence among heterospecific species combinations.

  • mm(population(_1), population(_2)) is a multi-membership random effect accounting for repeated use of the same populations across pairwise comparisons.

  • mm(individual(_1), individual(_2)) is a multi-membership random effect accounting for repeated use of the same individuals across pairwise comparisons.

Model specifications:

  • The model was fitted in a Bayesian framework using the brms package, with sympatry as the only fixed effect. Geographic distance was not included: sympatric comparisons are all within-locality and allopatric comparisons all between localities, so sympatry and geographic distance cannot be separated with this sampling (posterior correlation between their coefficients of about 0.9 in the models that included both). Because isolation by distance predicts greater similarity at short distances, omitting geographic distance should, if anything, make the sympatry effect conservative when acoustic distance increases with geographic distance.
  • The response variable was pairwise Euclidean acoustic distance and was modeled using a Gaussian error distribution.
  • Only heterospecific comparisons were included.
  • Analyses were restricted to species pairs for which both sympatric and allopatric population comparisons were available. This restriction ensured that sympatry effects were estimated within the same species-pair contrasts rather than being confounded by species pairs occurring exclusively in sympatry or exclusively in allopatry.
  • Species-pair identity was included as a random intercept to account for inherent differences in acoustic divergence among species combinations.
  • Population identity was modeled using a multi-membership random effect because each pairwise comparison simultaneously involves two populations, and each population contributes to multiple pairwise distances.
  • Individual identity was modeled using a multi-membership random effect because each pairwise comparison simultaneously involves two individuals, and each individual contributes to multiple pairwise distances.
  • Only unique pairwise comparisons were retained and self-comparisons were excluded.
  • Weakly informative, regularizing priors were specified for all parameters.
  • The model was fitted using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo as implemented in Stan through the cmdstanr backend.

The coefficient for sympatry in this model represents the difference in acoustic divergence between sympatric (within-locality) and allopatric (between-locality) heterospecific comparisons, after accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data. The same model structure was fitted separately for the overall PCA-based acoustic distance and for each individual acoustic feature, so that trait-specific effects of sympatry could be examined alongside the overall pattern.

2.1.1.1.5 Divergent transitions

The sampler produced divergent transitions in this model. Divergences mark regions of the posterior that Hamiltonian Monte Carlo could not explore reliably, so estimates from those regions may be biased (Betancourt 2017). Without refitting, the checks below show (1) how many post-warmup draws diverged and whether they are concentrated in one chain, (2) whether divergences pile up at small values of any random-effect SD (a “funnel”; the species-pair SD, estimated from only three levels, is the most likely candidate), and (3) whether the fixed-effect estimates differ between divergent and non-divergent draws. If divergences are confined to a random-effect SD and the fixed effects look the same in both sets of draws, the inference on sympatry is unlikely to be affected. These checks cannot show what the sampler failed to explore, so refitting (e.g. with species pair as a fixed effect) remains the definitive test.

2.1.1.1.6 Contrasts among population pairs

Using the fitted model as is, the expected acoustic distance is computed for every observed combination of populations within each species pair, including the species-pair and population (multi-membership) effects and setting the individual effects to zero (i.e. for an average individual). Sympatric population pairs (the two species recorded at the same locality) are then contrasted against each allopatric population pair of the same species pair; each contrast is computed draw by draw, so its uncertainty interval reflects the joint posterior. Contrasts that share a population pair (e.g. the same sympatric pair against several allopatric ones) are not independent of each other and should be read as a set rather than as separate tests.

Model summary
  • Of the 8 trait-level models, 6 have credible sympatry effects. Element-level PCA distance shows by far the largest effect (1.279 SD, 95% CI [1.252, 1.306]) — an order of magnitude larger than any single song-level feature. Among song-level features, frequency range (0.038, 95% CI [0.001, 0.075]), gap duration (0.063, 95% CI [0.030, 0.097]) and element diversity/mst (0.044, 95% CI [0.010, 0.078]) are credibly positive (greater distance in sympatry), while peak frequency (-0.063, 95% CI [-0.097, -0.029]) and song rate (-0.082, 95% CI [-0.116, -0.049]) are credibly negative (smaller distance in sympatry). Number of elements (0.000) and song duration (0.005) show no credible effect.
  • Divergent transitions stayed below 2% for every feature, and the sympatry coefficient is essentially unchanged between divergent and non-divergent draws in every model, so none of these conclusions hinge on the divergences.
Interpretation
  • The character-displacement signal in complex songs is concentrated at the fine-grained element level: element-level acoustic distance is massively amplified in sympatry, far beyond what the overall song-level PCA-based model (0.042 SD) or any single song-level feature shows. Averaging element-level detail into song-level descriptors appears to dilute a strong underlying signal.
  • Song-level features show a mosaic rather than uniform divergence: gap duration, frequency range and element diversity/mst diverge modestly in sympatry (consistent with selection against acoustic confusion), but peak frequency and song rate actually converge in sympatry — heterospecific pairs sound more alike in pace and dominant frequency where they co-occur than where they do not.
  • This convergence in frequency and rate is the opposite of classical character displacement and is more consistent with shared ecological/acoustic constraints in sympatry (e.g. adaptation to the same habitat acoustics, morphological convergence in body size, or selection on these particular features for interspecific territorial signalling) acting alongside divergence in other traits. Complex-song divergence in sympatry is therefore trait-specific rather than a wholesale shift of the song.

# Simple songs

## Statistical analysis

To evaluate whether acoustic divergence between heterospecific songs differed between sympatric and allopatric population comparisons, we fitted a Bayesian mixed-effects model while accounting for the non-independence inherent to pairwise distance data.

The model was specified as:

$$
\begin{split}
\text{acoustic distance} &\sim \text{sympatry} \\
&\quad + (1 \mid \text{species pair}) \\
&\quad + (1 \mid \text{mm(population}_1,\text{population}_2)) \\
&\quad + (1 \mid \text{mm(individual}_1,\text{individual}_2))
\end{split}
$$

where:

-   \(\text{acoustic distance}_{ij}\) is the Euclidean distance between songs \(i\) and \(j\) in multivariate acoustic space.

-   \(\text{sympatry}_{ij}\) is a binary predictor indicating whether the populations from which songs \(i\) and \(j\) were recorded occur in sympatry (1) or allopatry (0).

-   species pair is a random intercept accounting for baseline differences in acoustic divergence among heterospecific species combinations.

-   mm(population\(_1\), population\(_2\)) is a multi-membership random effect accounting for repeated use of the same populations across pairwise comparisons.

-   mm(individual\(_1\), individual\(_2\)) is a multi-membership random effect accounting for repeated use of the same individuals across pairwise comparisons.

Model specifications:

-   The model was fitted in a Bayesian framework using the brms package, with sympatry as the only fixed effect. Geographic distance was not included: sympatric comparisons are all within-locality and allopatric comparisons all between localities, so sympatry and geographic distance cannot be separated with this sampling (posterior correlation between their coefficients of about 0.9 in the models that included both). Because isolation by distance predicts greater similarity at short distances, omitting geographic distance should, if anything, make the sympatry effect conservative when acoustic distance increases with geographic distance.
-   The response variable was pairwise Euclidean acoustic distance and was modeled using a Gaussian error distribution.
-   Only heterospecific comparisons were included.
-   Analyses were restricted to species pairs for which both sympatric and allopatric population comparisons were available. This restriction ensured that sympatry effects were estimated within the same species-pair contrasts rather than being confounded by species pairs occurring exclusively in sympatry or exclusively in allopatry.
-   Species-pair identity was included as a random intercept to account for inherent differences in acoustic divergence among species combinations.
-   Population identity was modeled using a multi-membership random effect because each pairwise comparison simultaneously involves two populations, and each population contributes to multiple pairwise distances.
-   Individual identity was modeled using a multi-membership random effect because each pairwise comparison simultaneously involves two individuals, and each individual contributes to multiple pairwise distances.
-   Only unique pairwise comparisons were retained and self-comparisons were excluded.
-   Weakly informative, regularizing priors were specified for all parameters.
-   The model was fitted using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo as implemented in Stan through the cmdstanr backend.

The coefficient for sympatry in this model represents the difference in acoustic divergence between sympatric (within-locality) and allopatric (between-locality) heterospecific comparisons, after accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data. The same model structure was fitted separately for the overall PCA-based acoustic distance and for each individual acoustic feature, so that trait-specific effects of sympatry could be examined alongside the overall pattern.

##### Divergent transitions

The sampler produced divergent transitions in this model. Divergences mark regions of the posterior that Hamiltonian Monte Carlo could not explore reliably, so estimates from those regions may be biased (Betancourt 2017). Without refitting, the checks below show (1) how many post-warmup draws diverged and whether they are concentrated in one chain, (2) whether divergences pile up at small values of any random-effect SD (a "funnel"; the species-pair SD, estimated from only three levels, is the most likely candidate), and (3) whether the fixed-effect estimates differ between divergent and non-divergent draws. If divergences are confined to a random-effect SD and the fixed effects look the same in both sets of draws, the inference on sympatry is unlikely to be affected. These checks cannot show what the sampler failed to explore, so refitting (e.g. with species pair as a fixed effect) remains the definitive test.

-   Sympatric heterospecific pairs have credibly greater PCA-based acoustic distance than allopatric pairs (sympatry1 = 0.227 SD, 95% CI [0.204, 0.250]), with clean convergence (Rhat = 1, no CIs near zero).

##### Contrasts among population pairs

Using the fitted model as is, the expected acoustic distance is computed for every observed combination of populations within each species pair, including the species-pair and population (multi-membership) effects and setting the individual effects to zero (i.e. for an average individual). Sympatric population pairs (the two species recorded at the same locality) are then contrasted against each allopatric population pair of the same species pair; each contrast is computed draw by draw, so its uncertainty interval reflects the joint posterior. Contrasts that share a population pair (e.g. the same sympatric pair against several allopatric ones) are not independent of each other and should be read as a set rather than as separate tests.

## Statistical analysis

To evaluate whether acoustic divergence between heterospecific songs differed between sympatric and allopatric population comparisons, we fitted a Bayesian mixed-effects model while accounting for the non-independence inherent to pairwise distance data.

The model was specified as:

$$
\begin{split}
\text{acoustic distance} &\sim \text{sympatry} \\
&\quad + (1 \mid \text{species pair}) \\
&\quad + (1 \mid \text{mm(population}_1,\text{population}_2)) \\
&\quad + (1 \mid \text{mm(individual}_1,\text{individual}_2))
\end{split}
$$

where:

-   \(\text{acoustic distance}_{ij}\) is the Euclidean distance between songs \(i\) and \(j\) in multivariate acoustic space.

-   \(\text{sympatry}_{ij}\) is a binary predictor indicating whether the populations from which songs \(i\) and \(j\) were recorded occur in sympatry (1) or allopatry (0).

-   species pair is a random intercept accounting for baseline differences in acoustic divergence among heterospecific species combinations.

-   mm(population\(_1\), population\(_2\)) is a multi-membership random effect accounting for repeated use of the same populations across pairwise comparisons.

-   mm(individual\(_1\), individual\(_2\)) is a multi-membership random effect accounting for repeated use of the same individuals across pairwise comparisons.

Model specifications:

-   The model was fitted in a Bayesian framework using the brms package, with sympatry as the only fixed effect. Geographic distance was not included: sympatric comparisons are all within-locality and allopatric comparisons all between localities, so sympatry and geographic distance cannot be separated with this sampling (posterior correlation between their coefficients of about 0.9 in the models that included both). Because isolation by distance predicts greater similarity at short distances, omitting geographic distance should, if anything, make the sympatry effect conservative when acoustic distance increases with geographic distance.
-   The response variable was pairwise Euclidean acoustic distance and was modeled using a Gaussian error distribution.
-   Only heterospecific comparisons were included.
-   Analyses were restricted to species pairs for which both sympatric and allopatric population comparisons were available. This restriction ensured that sympatry effects were estimated within the same species-pair contrasts rather than being confounded by species pairs occurring exclusively in sympatry or exclusively in allopatry.
-   Species-pair identity was included as a random intercept to account for inherent differences in acoustic divergence among species combinations.
-   Population identity was modeled using a multi-membership random effect because each pairwise comparison simultaneously involves two populations, and each population contributes to multiple pairwise distances.
-   Individual identity was modeled using a multi-membership random effect because each pairwise comparison simultaneously involves two individuals, and each individual contributes to multiple pairwise distances.
-   Only unique pairwise comparisons were retained and self-comparisons were excluded.
-   Weakly informative, regularizing priors were specified for all parameters.
-   The model was fitted using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo as implemented in Stan through the cmdstanr backend.

The coefficient for sympatry in this model represents the difference in acoustic divergence between sympatric (within-locality) and allopatric (between-locality) heterospecific comparisons, after accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data. The same model structure was fitted separately for the overall PCA-based acoustic distance and for each individual acoustic feature, so that trait-specific effects of sympatry could be examined alongside the overall pattern.

##### Divergent transitions

The sampler produced divergent transitions in this model. Divergences mark regions of the posterior that Hamiltonian Monte Carlo could not explore reliably, so estimates from those regions may be biased (Betancourt 2017). Without refitting, the checks below show (1) how many post-warmup draws diverged and whether they are concentrated in one chain, (2) whether divergences pile up at small values of any random-effect SD (a "funnel"; the species-pair SD, estimated from only three levels, is the most likely candidate), and (3) whether the fixed-effect estimates differ between divergent and non-divergent draws. If divergences are confined to a random-effect SD and the fixed effects look the same in both sets of draws, the inference on sympatry is unlikely to be affected. These checks cannot show what the sampler failed to explore, so refitting (e.g. with species pair as a fixed effect) remains the definitive test.

##### Summary {.unnumbered .unlisted}

###### Model summary {.unnumbered .unlisted}

-   Of the 8 trait-level models, 6 have credible sympatry effects. Element-level PCA distance shows by far the largest effect (1.279 SD, 95% CI [1.252, 1.306]) — an order of magnitude larger than any single song-level feature. Among song-level features, frequency range (0.038, 95% CI [0.001, 0.075]), gap duration (0.063, 95% CI [0.030, 0.097]) and element diversity/mst (0.044, 95% CI [0.010, 0.078]) are credibly *positive* (greater distance in sympatry), while peak frequency (-0.063, 95% CI [-0.097, -0.029]) and song rate (-0.082, 95% CI [-0.116, -0.049]) are credibly *negative* (smaller distance in sympatry). Number of elements (0.000) and song duration (0.005) show no credible effect.
-   Divergent transitions stayed below 2% for every feature, and the sympatry coefficient is essentially unchanged between divergent and non-divergent draws in every model, so none of these conclusions hinge on the divergences.

###### Interpretation {.unnumbered .unlisted}

-   The character-displacement signal in complex songs is concentrated at the fine-grained element level: element-level acoustic distance is massively amplified in sympatry, far beyond what the overall song-level PCA-based model (0.042 SD) or any single song-level feature shows. Averaging element-level detail into song-level descriptors appears to dilute a strong underlying signal.
-   Song-level features show a mosaic rather than uniform divergence: gap duration, frequency range and element diversity/mst diverge modestly in sympatry (consistent with selection against acoustic confusion), but peak frequency and song rate actually *converge* in sympatry — heterospecific pairs sound more alike in pace and dominant frequency where they co-occur than where they do not.
-   This convergence in frequency and rate is the opposite of classical character displacement and is more consistent with shared ecological/acoustic constraints in sympatry (e.g. adaptation to the same habitat acoustics, morphological convergence in body size, or selection on these particular features for interspecific territorial signalling) acting alongside divergence in other traits. Complex-song divergence in sympatry is therefore trait-specific rather than a wholesale shift of the song.
